# How to Give and Receive Design Critique

## Research & Learn

**What is design critique, and why is it a crucial part of the UX process?**  
Design critique is when designers share their work and get feedback to help improve it. It’s not about judging the designer — it’s about making the design clearer, more usable, and more aligned with user needs. Critique helps catch issues early, strengthens teamwork, and keeps the design moving in the right direction.

**What are the key principles of giving helpful, constructive feedback?**  
Good feedback is specific, clear, and focused on the design, not the person. It should explain what works, what doesn’t, and why. Instead of saying “this looks bad,” it’s more helpful to say what feels confusing or what might cause problems for users. The goal is to support the designer, not discourage them.

**How should designers receive and apply critique without taking it personally?**  
Designers should listen openly, ask questions, and try to understand the reasoning behind the feedback. It helps to remember that critique is about improving the work, not judging their abilities. Taking notes, asking for examples, and checking feedback against user needs makes it easier to apply changes calmly and confidently.

**What are common mistakes when giving or receiving design feedback?**  
Common mistakes include giving vague comments, focusing on personal taste, or being overly negative. On the receiving side, reacting defensively or ignoring feedback can slow progress. Another mistake is trying to fix everything at once instead of prioritizing what matters most for the user.

## Reflection

**What is the difference between personal opinion and objective UX critique?**  
Personal opinion is based on someone’s taste — like preferring a certain color or style. Objective UX critique focuses on how well the design works for users. UX critique looks at clarity, usability, accessibility, and whether the design supports the user’s goals. It’s about function, not preference.

**If someone gives vague feedback like "I don’t like this", how would you get more useful input?**  
I’d ask follow‑up questions like “What part feels unclear?” or “What do you think users might struggle with?” Getting them to explain the reason behind their reaction usually leads to more helpful feedback. Sometimes people know something feels off but need a little guidance to express why.

**How can you ensure that design critique sessions are productive rather than overwhelming?**  
Keeping the session focused on a few key areas helps a lot. Setting expectations, staying respectful, and giving designers time to explain their decisions makes the conversation smoother. It also helps to keep feedback specific and actionable instead of piling on too many comments at once. A calm, structured approach keeps critique useful instead of stressful.
