# Key UX Challenges & Considerations for Neurodivergent Users

## Research & Learn

**What common UX barriers affect neurodivergent users?**
A few things come up a lot: too many decisions on one screen, unclear next steps, cluttered layouts, and apps that assume you'll just remember what you were doing after getting interrupted. For people with ADHD, figuring out what to do next is already tiring on its own, so if an interface doesn't make the next step obvious, that's extra friction before the task even starts. For autistic users, inconsistent or unpredictable layouts can be just as draining, since every screen has to be figured out from scratch instead of feeling familiar.

**How can predictability, clear feedback, and reduced distractions improve usability?**
When something's predictable, you spend less energy figuring out where things are, so there's more left over for the actual task. Clear feedback, like showing progress or confirming something worked, gets rid of that nagging "did that actually do anything?" feeling, which otherwise pulls focus away. And cutting distractions matters a lot for ADHD especially, gentle reminders alone don't really work, distractions need to actually be blocked.

**Why do overly complex interfaces cause issues for people with ADHD?**
Every extra choice or confusing bit on a screen costs a bit of attention, and ADHD brains already spend more effort just staying on task in the first place. A cluttered screen means constantly re-deciding what to click, where to look, what actually matters, which eats into the same attention that should be going toward the work itself. Simpler, more guided interfaces cut that overhead down.

**What are some real-world design examples that improve accessibility for neurodivergent users?**
Focus Bear is a good example. It shows the day visually instead of expecting you to remember or plan it yourself, and its break reminders are call-aware so they don't interrupt at a bad moment. More broadly, things like step-by-step routines instead of an open-ended to-do list, visual timelines instead of plain text, and blockers that take real effort to bypass instead of one click, all show up a lot in neurodivergent-friendly design.

## Reflection

**How can Focus Bear's design choices make tasks easier for neurodivergent users?**
Showing routines step by step instead of leaving someone to plan their morning from scratch cuts down a lot of the "what do I do next" fatigue. Blocking distractions across every device at once also means there's no easy way to just scroll somewhere else instead, which matters since soft reminders alone don't really work.

**If a feature is technically useful but cognitively overwhelming, how should it be redesigned?**
I'd strip it back to the one most important action and tuck everything else behind a second step, instead of showing all the options at once. Breaking it into smaller stages, one clear decision per screen, usually helps more than just tidying up the visuals. A good test is seeing if the feature still works with half of what's currently on screen removed, if it does, that's probably the version worth shipping.

**What are some small UX adjustments that can significantly improve user experience for neurodivergent users?**
Things like cutting down the number of choices on screen, keeping layouts consistent so nothing has to be re-learned every time, showing clear progress, and making the next step obvious without having to think about it. None of these are big redesigns, but together they save a lot of mental effort that would otherwise go into just figuring out the interface.
