# Introduction to Wireframing

## Research & Learn

**What is a wireframe, and how does it differ from a prototype?**
A wireframe is a simple, low-detail layout showing where things go on a screen, like a rough sketch of structure and content, without real colors, images, or styling. A prototype is more advanced, it's usually clickable or interactive, showing how a user would actually move through the product, sometimes with real visuals included.

**What are the key differences between low-fidelity and high-fidelity wireframes?**
Low-fidelity wireframes are quick and rough, often just boxes and lines to show layout and structure, without worrying about exact spacing or color. High-fidelity wireframes are more detailed and closer to the final look, sometimes including real text, spacing, and near-final visual choices, but still not a full prototype.

**When should wireframes be used in the UX design process?**
Early on, before any visual design work starts. Wireframes are for figuring out layout, structure, and user flow first, so decisions about content and functionality get made before time gets spent on colors, fonts, or polish.

**How do wireframes improve collaboration between UX, PMs, and developers?**
They give everyone something simple and fast to react to before too much effort has gone in. A PM can check if the flow matches business needs, and a developer can spot technical issues early, all without waiting for a fully designed screen. It also means feedback happens on structure, not just visuals, at a stage where changes are still cheap to make.

## Reflection

**What are the benefits of starting with wireframes rather than jumping into high-fidelity design?**
It's much faster to make changes to a wireframe than to a polished design, so problems with layout or flow get caught before a lot of time is invested. It also keeps early feedback focused on whether the structure makes sense, rather than getting sidetracked by opinions on color or style too soon.

**How can wireframes help identify usability issues early?**
Since wireframes strip away visual polish, they force attention onto whether the flow and layout actually make sense on their own. If something's confusing even in a simple wireframe, it's a sign there's a structural problem, not just a visual one, and that's much easier and cheaper to fix at this stage.

**If a stakeholder asks for a high-fidelity design too soon, how would you explain the value of wireframing?**
I'd explain that wireframes let us test whether the idea actually works before spending time making it look good, since fixing a layout problem is quick at this stage but expensive once it's fully designed. Basically, wireframing saves time overall, even though it looks like an extra step upfront.
