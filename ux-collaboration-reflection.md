# How UX Works with Other Teams

## Research & Learn

**How do UX designers work with developers to ensure accurate implementation?**
It's mostly about a clear handoff and sticking around after it, not disappearing once a design is "done." That means labeling designs with spacing, different states, and edge cases, not just the perfect-case screen, and being around to answer questions once development actually starts, since things always come up that a static mockup didn't cover. Tools like Figma that let developers inspect the design directly help too, so less gets lost along the way.

**How does UX contribute to product decisions and roadmap planning?**
UX usually brings the user's side into planning, what's actually worth solving, based on research instead of guesses. That means sometimes pushing back when a feature sounds good on paper but doesn't really solve a real problem, and helping the PM weigh ideas not just by business impact but by how much friction it removes for the user.

**What role does UX play in QA and usability testing?**
UX is usually the one running usability testing, watching real people use the product and seeing where they get stuck. That's different from QA, which is more about checking that something works the way it was built. UX is checking whether it actually makes sense to a person using it, which sometimes finds problems that technically pass QA but still confuse people.

**How should designers provide clear documentation and feedback to other teams?**
Documentation should explain why, not just what, why a decision was made, not just what was decided. Feedback lands better when it's specific and tied to a real user problem instead of just personal opinion, "this needs to change because users got stuck here" works a lot better than "I just don't like it."

## Reflection

**How can UX ensure a smooth handoff between design and development?**
Staying available after handing it off instead of treating it as a one-time thing. Most confusion happens when a developer hits something the design didn't cover and has to guess, so being reachable to answer questions quickly stops a lot of small inconsistencies before they happen.

**What challenges might arise when design, dev, and product teams have conflicting priorities?**
Usually dev wants simpler scope, product wants speed or impact, and UX wants the best experience for the user, and those don't always line up. The real risk is everyone just pushing their own side without explaining why, which turns into a standoff instead of an actual conversation about trade-offs.

**If a developer modifies a UX design due to technical limitations, how should a UX designer respond?**
Start by understanding the actual constraint before reacting to the change itself, there's usually a real reason behind it. Then look for a version that still solves the core problem even if the visual solution has to change. It's less about defending the original design and more about making sure whatever ships still does what it was meant to do.
